1. Introduction
    The association of frequencies with subspaces of a linear vector space is
an idea familiar from the modal theory of dynamical systems. For the case
of forced linear systems such an association has been formally established
by the introduction of the concept of (A, B)-invariant and controllability
subspaces by Wonham and Morse (1970), Wonham (1974). Following the
classical modal approach, (A, B)-invariant subspaces are thought of as direct
sums of simpler (A, B)-invariant subspaces, each of which is associated with
the transmission of a single frequency (Karcanias 1976, Karcanias and
Kouvaritakis 1978). The constituent (A, B)-invariant subspaces are known
as candidate closed-loop eigenspaces (Kimura 1975, Karcanias 1976, Moore
1976, Shaked and Karcanias 1976) which may have either simple or Jordan
structure. The use of matrix pencils (Karcanias 1978), provides an algebraic
tool which generalizes the Jordan canonical form, associated with the modal
decomposition of A-invariant subspaces, to the class of (A, B)-invariant sub-
spaces. The fundamental dynamical problem behind the concept of an
(A, B)-invariant subspace "F' is that of restricting the state trajectory to
"F' by using a finite control input; the modal decomposition of such subspaces
involves finite frequencies.
    Recently the concept of (A, B)-invariance was generalized bytwo different
approaches. The first (MacFarlane and Karcanias 1978) considers the fre-
quency propagation aspects of (A, B)-invariant subspaces, and using the tools
of matrix pencils defines the concept of an infinite spectrum (A, B)-invariant
subspace, as a subspace whose restriction pencil is characterized by infinite
elementary divisors. The second approach (Willems 1979) starts from the
dynamic characterization of (A, B)-invariance; by relaxing the condition

8.   Conclusions
    It was shown that the subspaces of the state space may be completely
characterized in geometric (vector space), algebraic (matrix pencil invariants),
dynamic (physical probJlems) and feedback (closed loop) terms. The restriction
pencil approach (Karcanias 1979) suggests naturally the way to extend the
concepts of (A, B)·-invariant subspaces (characterized by f.e.d. and c.m.i.)
and c.s. to that of infinite spectrum (A, B)-invariant subspaces (characterized
by i.e.d.). The relationship between the subspaces characterized by f.e.d.,
c.m.i. and i.e.d. and the concepts of almost (A, B)-invariant and almost c.s.
(Willems 1979) was established. It was shown that almost (A, B)-invariant
subspaces are those characterized hy f.e.d., c.m.i. and i.e.d. and that almost c.s.
are those characterized by c.m.i. and i.e.d. only and vice-versa; what Willems
calls ' coasting' and ' sliding' subspaces were shown to be subspaces charac-
terized only by f.e.d. and i.e.d. respectively. Furthermore, it was shown that
subspaces associated with i.e.d. are limits of generalized closed-loop eigenspaces
of infinitely large frequency and as such they were called 'asymptotic' or
infinite spectrum subspaces. The distinctive feature of asymptotic (sliding)
                                 Almost (A, B)·-invariant subspacee                                   89

subspaces with respect to the classical notion of (A, B)-illllariance is that they
are characterized by unbounded norm feedback and unbounded spectrum; it
is such properties which make the asymptotic subspaces of crucial importance
in the root locus analysis, which was briefly discussed in the final section.
