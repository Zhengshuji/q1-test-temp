Abstract
We study the computational complexity of static output feedback (SOF) stabilization
for continuous-time linear systems. For rational plant data, we prove that this problem
is complete for the existential theory of the reals: it is equivalent, under polynomial-time
transformations, to deciding whether a system of polynomial equations and inequalities with
integer coefficients has a real solution. This result holds even for controllable and observable
integer plants with two inputs. We characterize the effective gain dimension as the minimum
number of linear combinations of gain entries that determine the characteristic polynomial
for every gain. When this dimension is fixed, both feasibility testing and the construction of
a rational stabilizing gain, whenever one exists, can be performed in polynomial time. SOF
stabilization also remains strongly NP-hard for a family of minimal single-input integer plants.
Every feasible plant in this family admits a stabilizing gain with entries in{−1, 1}. For these
gains, stability is preserved under simultaneous perturbations of all entries, provided that
each perturbation has magnitude at most a uniform positive constant independent of the
plant dimensions.
Keywords:static output feedback; stabilization; computational complexity; existential theory
of the reals; controller synthesis.
1 Introduction
Static output feedback (SOF) uses a constant gain acting on measured outputs without introduc-
ing controller states. For continuous-time linear systems, the stabilization problem asks whether
such a real gain can make the closed-loop matrix Hurwitz. Understanding the computational
limits of this architecture helps assess what general synthesis methods can achieve and which
system structures make exact design tractable. These questions have motivated extensive work
on SOF (Syrmos et al., 1997; Sadabadi & Peaucelle, 2016).
Existing methods approach SOF through algebraic conditions on the gain and stability
certificates. Algebraic decision methods establish decidability (Anderson et al., 1975), and
quantifier elimination can be used for stabilization and pole placement (Röbenack & Voßwinkel,
2020). Other approaches exploit polynomial structure through convergent relaxations of matrix
inequalities (Henrion & Lasserre, 2006) or numerical algebraic geometry with exact certification
(Menini et al., 2025). A complexity classification addresses the underlying feasibility problem
and helps distinguish the difficulty of a particular procedure from that of the problem itself.
Incomplexityanalysis, unrestrictedstabilizationandstabilizationunderadditionalconstraints
are distinct problems (Blondel & Tsitsiklis, 2000). Blondel and Tsitsiklis (1997) consider
stabilization with prescribed intervals on gain entries, among other problems. Fu and Luo (1997)

The general complexity question also raises the question of which structural features permit
efficient exact synthesis. With a single scalar gain, stabilizing values can be characterized by
intervals determined by polynomial matrix zeros (Henrion et al., 2002). For matrix gains, counting
all gain entries may obscure such tractable cases because some directions preserve the closed-loop
characteristic polynomial for every gain. Existing studies of output feedback invariants and
canonical forms concern equivalence classes of systems (Ravi et al., 2002; Yannakoudakis, 2019).
Here the plant is fixed, and the relevant question is which gain directions can be removed without
losing any attainable characteristic polynomial. Answering this question gives a measure of the
number of variables needed for exact synthesis.
Westudyrationalplantsencodedinbinaryandunrestrictedrealgains. Convertingpolynomial
feasibility into stabilization requires addressing two obstacles. First, the characteristic polynomial
is affine in each gain entry, which restricts how polynomial constraints can be encoded. Second,
gains of unbounded magnitude may produce arbitrarily small constraint violations even when
the encoded equations have no solution. Our construction represents the polynomial constraints
through the closed-loop characteristic polynomial. Quantitative separation bounds ensure that
approximate solutions of an infeasible equation system cannot yield a stabilizing gain. The

The effective gain dimensionκ is the minimum number of linear combinations of gain
entries needed to determine the characteristic polynomial for every gain. For fixedκ, this
yields polynomial-time exact decision and rational synthesis, even with many redundant
gain entries. The bit complexity is(L + 1)O(1)(n + 1)O(κ+1), whereL is the input length

Concurrent preprints by Löfberg (2026) and Barkai and Shames (2026) establish NP-hardness
of unrestricted SOF stabilization via Betweenness and Subset Sum, respectively. Ahmadi et al.
(2026) establish strong NP-hardness via 3-SAT in both continuous and discrete time, with
their continuous-time single-output result also yielding single-input hardness by transposition.
Their continuous-time proof uses matrix definiteness and Lyapunov-type conditions, whereas
our single-input proof uses frequency separation and polynomial root counts. The present paper
addresses the completeness classification of SOF stabilization and the role of effective gain
dimension in exact synthesis.

2 Problem formulation
2.1 System model and stabilization problem
Consider the continuous-time linear system
˙x=Ax+Bu, y=Cx,
where x∈R n, u∈R m, andy∈R p are the state, input, and measured output, respectively.
The matricesA∈R n×n, B∈R n×m, andC∈R p×n are real, with positive dimensions. Under
static output feedbacku =Ky, the closed-loop matrix isA +BKC. A real square matrixM is
Hurwitz whenα(M)< 0. A minimal realization has the fewest states among realizations of the
same transfer matrix. Equivalently, it is controllable and observable.
Problem 1.The SOF stabilization decision problem, denoted bySOF, asks whether a given
real plant(A,B,C)admits a real gainK∈R m×p such thatA+BKCis Hurwitz.
2.2 Input encoding and complexity concepts
For the complexity analysis, plant matrices have rational entries, while gains may be real. Every
matrix entry, including zeros, is recorded explicitly as an integer numerator and a positive
integer denominator, both encoded in binary. The total input length isL ≥2. Running
times count elementary operations on binary digits, called bit operations, and thus account for
the dependence of exact arithmetic costs on the lengths of the numbers involved. A control
perspective on these concepts is given by Blondel and Tsitsiklis (2000, Sections 2.1 and 2.4).
Suppose that each input of decision problem A can be converted, in polynomial time, into
an input of decision problem B with the same YES or NO answer. We can then solve A by first
performing this conversion and then applying an algorithm for B.
Such a conversion, when performed by a deterministic algorithm, is called a polynomial-time
many-one reduction (Arora & Barak, 2009, Chapter 2). Time is measured in terms of the
encoded input length. The original input is called the source instance. All reductions below are
of this type.
Real polynomial feasibility asks whether there existsx∈Rd satisfying
fi(x) = 0,i= 1,...,r, g j(x)≥0,j= 1,...,s,
where fi and gj are polynomials with integer coefficients. Each polynomial is represented by
an explicit list of its nonzero monomials, with coefficients and nonnegative integer exponents
encoded in binary.
Definition 1.The class∃R consists of decision problems reducible to real polynomial feasibility.
A problem is∃R-complete if it belongs to∃R and real polynomial feasibility reduces to it
(Schaefer et al., 2026).
Definition 2.A decision problem is in NP if its YES inputs, and only those inputs, admit
certificates accepted by a deterministic verifier, with certificate length and verification time
bounded by polynomials in the input length. A problem is NP-hard if every problem inNP
reduces to it (Arora & Barak, 2009, Chapter 2).
For integer plant data, strong NP-hardness means that NP-hardness persists when the
absolute values of all matrix entries are bounded by a polynomial in the plant dimensions.

polynomial in the dimensions and the largest absolute matrix entry (Ahmadi et al., 2026).
SinceNP⊆∃R, every∃R-complete problem is NP-hard (Schaefer et al., 2026, Section 1.2).

3 Main results
3.1 Complexity classification and exact synthesis
Theorem 1.The decision problemSOF with rational plant data is∃R-complete under determin-
istic polynomial-time many-one reductions. It remains∃R-complete when restricted to minimal
integer plants with two inputs.
Theorem 1 places SOF stabilization at the same computational difficulty as deciding whether
a system of polynomial equations and inequalities has a real solution. Either problem can be
converted to the other in polynomial time. Consequently, unlessP =NP, no exact algorithm
can decide all SOF instances in time polynomial in the input length. This difficulty persists for
controllable and observable plants with only two inputs. The proof is given in Section 4. The
next theorem concerns the effective gain dimensionκdefined in Section 2.

Theorem 2.There exist an absolute constantc≥ 1and a deterministic exact algorithm that
decides SOF stabilization for rational plant data within
(L+ 1)c(n+ 1)c(κ+1) (2)
bit operations. On a feasible input, it returns a rational stabilizing gain.
With the numberκ of effective gain parameters fixed, Theorem 2 gives a polynomial-time
method to decide feasibility and construct a stabilizing gain, even as the state dimension grows.
In particular, the result applies when the input and output dimensions are fixed, sinceκ≤mp .
Section 5.1 gives the algorithm and proves the complexity bound. Appendix B.2 augments the
algorithm with an independently verifiable Lyapunov certificate and a bound on gain errors that
preserve stability, within the complexity bound (2).
3.2 Strong NP-hardness for single-input plants
Theorem 1 establishes hardness with two inputs. A single-input plant can still have a growing
number of outputs, so its gain need not have a fixed number of parameters. The next result gives
a family of minimal integer plants for which this hardness holds together with binary stabilizing
gains and a uniform tolerance to gain perturbations.
Theorem 3.SOF stabilization is strongly NP-hard for minimal single-input integer plants.
There exist a constantδ0> 0independent of the source instance and a polynomial-time reduction
from 3-SAT to this class with the following property. Each feasible plant produced by the reduction,
with v outputs, admits a gainK∗∈{− 1, 1}1×v such thatA +B(K∗ +D)C is Hurwitz for every
D∈R 1×v satisfying∥D∥ max≤δ 0.
Remark1.Theorem 3 shows that strong NP-hardness persists even when feasible instances
admit stabilizing gains with a simple representation and a positive tolerance to simultaneous
errors in all gain entries that is independent of the plant dimensions. Section 5.2 explains the
reduction, and Appendix C gives its construction and quantitative bounds.

6 Conclusion
In this paper, we studied the computational complexity of SOF stabilization for rational plants.
The general decision problem is∃R-complete even for minimal plants with two inputs, while
fixing the effective gain dimensionκ permits polynomial-time exact decision and synthesis. By
contrast, stabilization remains strongly NP-hard for minimal single-input plants, whose transfer
normal rank is one. Thus the tractability result depends on how many independent linear
combinations of gain entries determine the closed-loop characteristic polynomial, not simply
on transfer rank. It remains open whether SOF stabilization at transfer normal rank one is
∃R-complete and whether the exponent of the input length in an exact algorithm can be made
independent ofκ.
