# Review of `13_spectral_structure.md`

The new report makes a substantial advance. The most important change is that the spectral obstruction is no longer just a rank condition to test: you now have (i) an exact source–target incidence criterion, (ii) a quotient generalized-eigenvector formulation, and (iii) a concrete algebraic sufficient condition for whole-family failure.

My overall assessment is:

1. The second gap-2 example is the strongest new result. It shows that the gap is not merely an isolated one-step pathology, and that Type I and Type III obstructions can coexist at different orders.
2. The single-\\(\lambda\\) certificate is a useful construction mechanism, but it is too special to be the main complexity reduction. Its value is as a gadget or a sufficient-condition theorem.
3. Q3 is the right next mathematical question, but it needs a sharper formulation. “Rank-one pencil feasibility” covers several quite different problems; some are easy, while others are hard. Your particular common-\\(\lambda\\), two-column condition is more structured than general determinantal feasibility.
4. I would not start with a fixed-\\((n,p,r)\\) hardness claim. Fixed state dimension is a much stronger target than fixed input/output dimensions, and your present \\(6\times6\\) construction does not by itself suggest that the entire decision problem is hard at that size.

I’ll separate what follows into conclusions supported by the report, mathematical deductions, and external complexity context. The report’s verification labels are retained: an exact computation on examples is not the same as a proof for a family.

## 1. Two logical points to tighten before using the new claims

There are two places where I would be more cautious than the report.

### 1.1 The deformation experiment does not yet prove global thinness

The one-parameter experiment is strong evidence that whole-family infeasibility is exceptional along that particular deformation. But the conclusion

> “whole-family infeasibility is a codimension-\\(\ge1\\) phenomenon in the data”

does not follow from a one-parameter slice and finitely many candidate checks.

To prove a codimension statement in the full data space, you need an algebraic or constructible description of the entire infeasible locus, or at least a theorem placing it inside a proper algebraic subset. The table in II.4 establishes that the tested deformation has one exceptional parameter value, assuming the positive entries are backed by exact feasible witnesses. It does not establish the dimension of the full locus.

There is a related direction-of-implication issue in II.6. You have shown a sufficient certificate:

\\[ \exists\lambda:\\;(\lambda I-A)V=K \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{whole-family failure}, \\]

under the stated hypotheses. If this certificate is described by polynomial equations, then its solution set is an algebraic subset of the data space. But because the certificate is sufficient rather than necessary, this does not imply that the entire whole-family-infeasible locus is contained in that algebraic subset. Your two exceptions in II.6 are precisely a warning: whole-family failure can occur without one common \\(\lambda\\).

I would therefore change the current conclusion to:

> The constructed single-\\(\lambda\\) mechanism is an explicitly described algebraic sufficient locus for whole-family infeasibility. The deformation experiment suggests that the phenomenon is exceptional, but the codimension of the full infeasible locus remains open.

That is still a meaningful result, and it avoids building a complexity argument on an unproved geometric conclusion.

### 1.2 The common-\\(\lambda\\) condition is simpler than general determinantal feasibility

For fixed \\(M_1,M_2\in\mathbb C^{4\times2}\\), the condition

\\[ \exists\lambda\in\mathbb C:\quad M_2=\lambda M_1 \\]

is just scalar proportionality of two known matrices. Vectorizing gives

\\[ \operatorname{vec}(M_2)=\lambda\operatorname{vec}(M_1). \\]

This can be tested directly by checking whether the two vectors are proportional, with the zero-vector case handled separately. Equivalently, one solves a system of linear equations in the single scalar \\(\lambda\\).

So the fixed-\\(\lambda\\)-certificate test itself is polynomial-time; it is not a candidate source of \\(\exists\mathbb R\\)-hardness. Its polynomial equations may be quadratic in the input entries, but that does not make their feasibility hard: the unknown scalar is only one-dimensional, and the equations have a special common-proportionality structure.

This distinction is important for Q3. Generalized MinRank asks for a parameter value at which a polynomial matrix has bounded rank; the literature studies determinantal ideals generated by minors, and complexity depends heavily on the structure and number of parameters.&#x20;

## 2. Q1 — Is a reduction still plausible, or should the target be feasibility?

A reduction is still plausible, but I would target the original synthesis question: does there exist at least one feasible augmentation? That is the natural decision problem underlying \\(q\_{\min}\\).

The important distinction is between:

- Fixed-candidate failure: there exists a witness \\(x,\lambda\\) for which the candidate fails.
- Whole-family failure: every admissible candidate has such a witness.
- Synthesis feasibility: there exists an admissible candidate for which no such witness exists.

Your quotient formulation makes the first item very concrete:

\\[ J\_{\mathcal Z}x\ne0,\qquad T\_{\mathcal Z}x=\lambda J\_{\mathcal Z}x. \\]

But the synthesis problem asks for a \\(\mathcal Z\\) for which this generalized eigenvector does not exist. The existential observability certificate from your earlier work is valuable here: it turns the fixed-candidate “for every \\(\lambda\\)” condition into a finite algebraic certificate. Thus, once the candidate subspace is included among the unknowns, feasibility has an existential algebraic formulation.

That gives a natural upper-bound direction: the augmentation-existence problem belongs to the appropriate existential-algebraic feasibility class, subject to the real/complex encoding convention already discussed. It does not imply hardness, but it tells you that you need not formulate the target as a universal problem over all candidates.

I would not infer from the apparent thinness of the infeasible examples that the feasible side is automatically the hard side. A thin exceptional set can still encode a difficult decision problem if the input is transformed so that the exceptional set is hit exactly when the source instance has a solution. Conversely, your current common-\\(\lambda\\) certificate is too special to serve as that encoding.

My recommendation for Q1: formulate the decision problem as

\\[ \textsc{Feasible-Augmentation}(A,C,L_0,q): \quad \exists\mathcal L\text{ satisfying (5a) and (5b)}. \\]

Then study whether its existential certificate can encode a known hard algebraic feasibility problem. Keep whole-family infeasibility as a structural subproblem, not the primary hardness target.

## 3. Q2 — Should you fix \\(n=6,p=r=2\\) and seek hardness there?

I would not make that the main hardness target.

There is a fundamental complexity-theoretic obstacle: if \\(n,p,r\\) are all fixed constants, then \\(q\le n-p\\) is also bounded, and the number of entries of the unknown augmentation \\(\mathcal L\\) is fixed. Your algebraic feasibility formulation then has a fixed number of real variables and bounded-degree polynomial constraints. Fixed-dimensional real algebraic feasibility can be decided in polynomial time in the coefficient bit-size, using fixed-dimensional quantifier-elimination methods. The \\(\exists\mathbb R\\) compendium explicitly records polynomial-time solvability when the number of variables is fixed.&#x20;

So a standard \\(\exists\mathbb R\\)-hardness result at the literal fixed size \\(n=6,p=r=2\\) would imply a major complexity collapse (in particular, \\(\exists\mathbb R\subseteq\mathrm P\\)). That is not a sensible first target.

There are three different meanings of “bounded size” worth distinguishing:

1. Fixed state and output dimensions: \\(n,p,r\\) are constants. This is unlikely to support standard uniform \\(\exists\mathbb R\\)-hardness for the reason above.
2. Fixed \\(p,r\\), variable \\(n\\): this is a meaningful restricted complexity problem. The number of augmentation variables grows with \\(n\\), so the fixed-variable obstruction disappears.
3. A reduction whose local gadget has size \\(6\\), but whose number of gadgets grows: this is also meaningful. The global instance has growing dimension even though every local obstruction is a copy of your smallest gadget.

I would pursue option 2 or 3, not option 1.

The \\(n=6,p=r=2\\) example is still valuable: it identifies a minimal local obstruction and may be the basic component of a scalable construction. But “smallest non-degenerate gadget” and “fixed-size hard problem” are very different claims.

## 4. Q3 — What is the right name for the quadratic condition, and is it hard?

There are three related problems here. They should not be conflated.

### 4.1 The exact condition in II.6: common scalar proportionality

The condition is

\\[ \exists\lambda\in\mathbb C:\quad M_2=\lambda M_1. \\]

For fixed matrices, this is easy. It is not general MinRank: there is no free vector of several parameters and no search over arbitrary low-rank matrices. It is a one-scalar proportionality test.

A clean algebraic description is obtained by setting

\\[ u=\operatorname{vec}(M_1),\qquad v=\operatorname{vec}(M_2). \\]

Then, provided \\(u\ne0\\),

\\[ v=\lambda u \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{rank}[u\ v]\le1. \\]

If \\(u=0\\), one must additionally require \\(v=0\\). This is a determinantal condition on a \\(8\times2\\) matrix, with the zero case treated separately.

Thus the most direct formulation is not “thirteen arbitrary quadratic equations”; it is a rank-one condition on a two-column matrix of vectorized data, plus the zero case. If the report's thirteen equations are a reduced description exploiting the special structure of \\(M_1,M_2\\), I would want an explicit proof that they are globally equivalent, including all zero-column and rank-deficient cases. The vectorized formulation is the safe reference formulation.

### 4.2 A genuine one-parameter rank-drop problem

A different problem is

\\[ \exists\lambda\in\mathbb C:\quad \operatorname{rank}(M_0+\lambda M_1)\le k. \\]

This is a univariate determinantal feasibility problem. It can be attacked by computing the gcd of the relevant minors as univariate polynomials, with degree and bit-complexity depending on the input representation. Your own exact one-variable gcd calculation in II.3 is an example of this kind of method.

This is much closer to a standard matrix-pencil rank-drop problem. It is still not automatically hard: one parameter is a severe restriction. The literature on generalized MinRank studies polynomial matrices and determinantal ideals, but the complexity results depend on the number of parameters, degree, and structural assumptions.&#x20;

### 4.3 Multi-parameter MinRank

The more promising hardness candidate is a family such as

\\[ M_0+x_1M_1+\cdots+x_tM_t \\]

with \\(t\\) growing, and the question

\\[ \exists x\in\mathbb C^t:\quad \operatorname{rank}(M_0+x_1M_1+\cdots+x_tM_t)\le k. \\]

This is a genuine parameterized determinantal-feasibility problem. It is the direction in which to look for known hardness results or reductions, rather than trying to extract hardness from one common scalar.

But there is a substantial missing bridge: your augmentation problem does not currently give an arbitrary multi-parameter affine matrix family. Its matrices arise from the constrained geometry of \\(A,C,L_0\\), the admissible \\(\mathcal Z\\), and the requirement that the same \\(\mathcal Z\\) work for the entire spectral test. You would need to show that this structure can encode the target MinRank instance.

My conclusion for Q3: the II.6 certificate is tractable and should be treated as a construction lemma, not as the hardness source. The next useful question is whether the variable-subspace condition

\\[ \exists\mathcal Z\in\mathrm{Gr}(d,K) \quad\text{such that}\quad \text{no }x,\lambda\text{ satisfy } x\notin\mathcal Z,\\;(\lambda I-A)x\in\mathcal Z \\]

can encode a multi-parameter determinantal problem. That is the actual structural bottleneck.

## 5. Q4 — Can the order gap be made arbitrarily large?

I think this is plausible, but I would not yet regard it as the most likely route to a complexity result.

The obvious construction is to combine several copies of the gap-1 or gap-2 gadgets. If the minimum feasible augmentation order were additive under a suitable direct-sum operation, then \\(k\\) copies of a gap-1 gadget would give a gap of \\(k\\), and \\(k\\) copies of the gap-2 gadget would give a gap of \\(2k\\).

The obstacle is that direct-sum additivity is not automatic. Even if

\\[ A=A_1\oplus A_2,\quad C=C_1\oplus C_2,\quad L_0=L\_{0,1}\oplus L\_{0,2}, \\]

an admissible augmentation \\(\mathcal L\\) need not be block diagonal. Its rows may mix state coordinates from different blocks. Such mixed rows might overcome an obstruction that is unavoidable in either block separately.

I would therefore split Q4 into two lemmas.

Lemma A — lower-bound additivity. Prove that every feasible augmentation of the combined system must contribute at least \\(q\_{\min}^{(i)}\\) independent rows to block \\(i\\), or establish a substitute inequality

\\[ q\_{\min}(1\oplus2)\ge q\_{\min}(1)+q\_{\min}(2). \\]

Lemma B — upper-bound additivity. Show that block-diagonal feasible augmentations combine, giving

\\[ q\_{\min}(1\oplus2)\le q\_{\min}(1)+q\_{\min}(2). \\]

If both hold, then the gap is additive. But Lemma A is the difficult one; it must rule out the benefit of mixed augmentations.

A possible way to attack it is to choose blocks with disjoint spectral data and derive a decomposition theorem for the quotient generalized-eigenvector relation. However, disjoint spectra alone do not prove that every admissible \\(\mathcal Z\\) decomposes blockwise. You would need to show that the relevant subspace relation respects the primary decomposition.

My recommendation: first try to prove additivity for two copies of your new gap-2 example. Do not immediately attempt a general \\(k\\)-block theorem. If even the two-copy lower bound fails, inspect the mixed augmentation: it may reveal a new mechanism that is more interesting than the gap construction itself.

## 6. Q5 — A verifiable tractable class

Yes. I would start with the quotient pencil, but first establish a particularly simple sufficient condition directly in the original spaces.

Recall that failure at \\(\mathcal Z\\) requires

\\[ x\in V\setminus\mathcal Z,\qquad (\lambda I-A)x\in\mathcal Z\subseteq K. \\]

Consequently, the following condition is sufficient for spectral feasibility of every admissible \\(\mathcal Z\subseteq K\\), at every order:

\\[ \boxed{ \ker\bigl((\lambda I-A)|\_V\bigr) \cap \\{x\in V:(\lambda I-A)x\in K\\} =\\{0\\} } \\]

for every \\(\lambda\\) is not quite the cleanest way to state it; a stronger and simpler condition is

\\[ \boxed{ (\lambda I-A)V\cap K=\\{0\\} \qquad\text{for every }\lambda\in\mathbb C. } \tag{T} \\]

Indeed, if failure occurred, then \\((\lambda I-A)x\\) would belong to both \\((\lambda I-A)V\\) and \\(K\\), and hence would be zero. Thus \\(Ax=\lambda x\\). To conclude there is no failure, one must additionally rule out nonzero eigenvectors \\(x\in V\setminus\mathcal Z\\). So (T) alone is not sufficient: a zero image is still in every \\(\mathcal Z\\). The correct strengthened condition is

\\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &(\lambda I-A)V\cap K=\\{0\\},\\\ &\ker((\lambda I-A)|\_V)=\\{0\\}, \end{aligned} \qquad \forall\lambda\in\mathbb C. } \tag{T'} \\]

The second line is actually already implied by the first, since \\(0\in K\\) and a vector in the kernel maps to zero. Therefore (T') reduces to (T), provided the first line is interpreted as excluding nonzero image vectors but the kernel is separately checked. More transparently, the condition I recommend implementing is:

\\[ \boxed{ (\lambda I-A)|\_V\text{ is injective, and } (\lambda I-A)V\cap K=\\{0\\} \quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb C. } \tag{T''} \\]

Under (T''), failure is impossible for any \\(\mathcal Z\subseteq K\\).

This is deliberately strong, but it is a clean, verifiable tractability certificate. To test it, choose a basis matrix \\(B_V\\) for \\(V\\), and let \\(P_K\\) be a full-row-rank matrix with kernel \\(K\\). Then check that the pencil

\\[ P_K(\lambda I-A)B_V \\]

has full column rank for every \\(\lambda\\), and separately that \\((\lambda I-A)B_V\\) has full column rank for every \\(\lambda\\). Both are univariate polynomial-matrix questions: compute the maximal minors and test whether they have a common complex root. Equivalently, test whether their gcd is constant, with the usual care about the number of rows and the possibility that all maximal minors vanish identically.

There is a subtle point here: if \\((\lambda I-A)|\_V\\) is injective and its image intersects \\(K\\) trivially, then the projected map \\(P_K(\lambda I-A)|\_V\\) is injective. Conversely, injectivity of that projected map already implies both properties. Thus the practical condition can be stated more compactly as

\\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}\bigl(P_K(\lambda I-A)B_V\bigr)=\dim V \quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb C. } \tag{T\*} \\]

This is the version I would use in code and in a theorem.

It is stronger than necessary, because a particular \\(\mathcal Z\\) may avoid all the nonzero images in \\(K\\), even when the whole image \\((\lambda I-A)V\\) intersects \\(K\\). But it is a genuine matrix-only sufficient condition, independent of the candidate augmentation.

### A more ambitious quotient-pencil direction

For a fixed \\(\mathcal Z\\), the exact condition is that the pair

\\[ (T\_{\mathcal Z},J\_{\mathcal Z}) \\]

has no generalized eigenvector with \\(J\_{\mathcal Z}x\ne0\\). This suggests a hierarchy of tractable classes:

- Uniform transversality: the projected pencil in (T\*) has full column rank for every \\(\lambda\\).
- Uniform quotient injectivity on a restricted family: the same rank condition holds only for the \\(\mathcal Z\\)'s satisfying your algebraic admissibility constraints.
- Uniform spectral separation: the generalized eigenvectors of the quotient pair are forced into \\(J\_{\mathcal Z}x=0\\), uniformly over that family.

The first is easy to state and verify. The second is potentially much more useful, but it brings back the Grassmannian quantification. The third may lead to a structural theorem if you can characterize the quotient pairs through \\(U_0,U_1\\).

I would not begin by seeking a weaker condition in terms of \\((U_0,U_1)\\) alone. Your examples already show that \\(\varepsilon=0\\) is not sufficient for whole-family failure, and that isolated Type III candidates can have \\(\varepsilon\ge1\\). The quotient pair retains more of the actual spectral information.

## 7. Q6 — Which thread should you pursue first?

My ranking is:

1\. Q3 — Identify the exact rank-feasibility problem

First

The first result should be a short theorem: the common-\\(\lambda\\) certificate is a polynomial-time scalar-proportionality test, not a general hard MinRank instance. Then isolate the genuinely difficult variable-subspace problem. This prevents spending weeks trying to reduce from a tractable special case.

2\. Q5 — Find a nontrivial tractable class

Second

The uniform projected-pencil criterion (T\*) gives you an immediate exact sufficient condition. Test how often it holds, whether it can be weakened using the admissible Grassmannian family, and whether it captures any nontrivial examples.

3\. Q4 — Try a two-copy gap construction

Third

The gap-2 example makes arbitrary gaps a worthwhile structural question. But the direct-sum lower bound is the key obstacle, so begin with two copies and actively search for mixed augmentations.

4\. Q2 — Fixed-size hardness

Drop as a hardness target

Keep \\(n=6,p=r=2\\) as a local gadget, but do not target standard hardness with all dimensions fixed. Instead, ask whether that gadget can be embedded repeatedly in a growing-dimensional instance.

## 8. A concrete next experiment

I would make the next computational/theoretical round answer these three questions, in this order:

1. Certificate complexity: implement the common-scalar test \\(M_2=\lambda M_1\\) directly, including all zero and rank-deficient cases. Compare it against the thirteen-equation implementation. This should expose any hidden assumptions in the reduced equations.
2. Tractable-class coverage: implement (T\*) exactly using rational polynomial gcds. Run it on your existing feasible and infeasible examples. In particular, report whether it holds on any nontrivial instances where the single-\\(\lambda\\) certificate does not apply.
3. Two-copy experiment: form the direct sum of the gap-2 example with itself. Compute \\(q_0\\), find the block-diagonal feasible augmentation, and then search for mixed augmentations of lower order. If the lower bound survives, try to prove the two-block additivity lemma.
