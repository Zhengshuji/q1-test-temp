# Academic review of `08_per_order_decision_report.md`

This report identifies an important structural issue in the per-order decision programme: the size of the maximal minors used in the nullspace-pencil test is itself parameter-dependent. The earlier Bézout coefficient-matrix formulation is valid only after the relevant rank has been fixed. Your new stratification by

\\[ \rho=\operatorname{rank}[V\mid Z(\theta)] \\]

therefore addresses a genuine mathematical issue, not merely an implementation detail.

My main recommendations are:

1. Q1: Replace the enumeration of individual witness minors by a single ideal saturation for each rank stratum. More importantly, encode \\((5a)\\) by a column-space inclusion with auxiliary variables. This may eliminate the second rank stratification altogether.
2. Q2: Try the implicit polynomial-matrix formulation first. Do not expand all maximal minors. For fixed augmentations, polynomial-matrix algorithms can test the spectral condition without enumerating the minors; the harder issue is the parameter-dependent existence problem.
3. Q3: The generic-gcd-degree dichotomy is not an invariant of \\((V,W)\\) alone. It is an invariant of a specified algebraic family, and must be studied component by component. Also, the report's claim that generic degree zero implies feasibility at every point needs a distinction between generic full rank and full rank everywhere.
4. Q4: The two conditions are not complementary. The \\((5a)\\) and spectral conditions impose different constraints. The degenerate point in the \\(n=6\\) example is entirely compatible with their independence.
5. Q5: Write up the two established results now. Continue the per-order decision work as a focused secondary programme, beginning with the improved algebraic formulation below.

I will first clarify the rank-stratification issue, because it affects the interpretation of the other four questions.

## 1. A necessary clarification: the Bézout matrix must be built at the correct rank

The report gives an important example: at a special parameter value,

\\[ \operatorname{rank}[V\mid Z(0)]=3, \\]

whereas the generic rank is \\(4\\). If the generic minor size is retained at that special point, the resulting coefficient matrix gives the wrong verdict. The Toeplitz and direct nullspace-pencil tests correctly report feasibility. The issue is not a failure of the Bézout identity; it is that the wrong family of minors was used. 【turn7file0】

The correct construction is as follows.

For a fixed parameter \\(\theta\\), put

\\[ \rho(\theta)=\operatorname{rank}[V\mid Z(\theta)]. \\]

The nullspace-pencil criterion concerns the rank-\\(\rho(\theta)\\) minors of

\\[ P\_{Z(\theta)}(\lambda) = [(\lambda I-A)V\mid Z(\theta)]. \\]

Let

\\[ m_1^{(\rho)}(\theta,\lambda),\ldots, m\_{N\_\rho}^{(\rho)}(\theta,\lambda) \\]

denote all \\(\rho\times\rho\\) minors, and let \\(D\_\rho\\) be a uniform upper bound on their degrees in \\(\lambda\\).

On the stratum

\\[ \mathcal S\_\rho = \\{\theta:\operatorname{rank}[V\mid Z(\theta)]=\rho\\}, \\]

the Bézout coefficient matrix \\(M\_\rho(\theta)\\) is constructed from this family of \\(\rho\\)-minors. Then, under the algebraic admissibility hypothesis required for the nullspace-pencil criterion,

\\[ (5b) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 1\in \operatorname{span} \\{\\,\lambda^k m_i^{(\rho)}(\theta,\lambda): 1\le i\le N\_\rho,\ 0\le k\<D\_\rho\\,\\}. \\]

Equivalently,

\\[ \boxed{ e_0\in\operatorname{col}M\_\rho(\theta). } \tag{1} \\]

This is a correct rank-stratum formulation.

There is one logical qualification I want to emphasize. The nullspace-pencil criterion was derived under \\((5a)\\). Thus, if a point fails \\((5a)\\), condition (1) by itself must not automatically be called equivalent to the original \\((5b)\\). At such a point, one should either verify the original pencil \\(Q\_{\mathcal L}(\lambda)\\) directly or state that one is testing the nullspace-pencil condition separately from the joint admissibility problem. This distinction becomes relevant to Q4.

## 2. Q1 — Can the double stratification be made cheaper?

Yes. I would try ideal saturation first, and I would also try to remove the second stratification by encoding \\((5a)\\) directly.

### 2.1 One saturation can replace the enumeration of witness minors

Let \\(R=\mathbb Q[\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k]\\) be the coordinate ring of a chart. For the matrix

\\[ B(\theta)=[V\mid Z(\theta)], \\]

write \\(I_j(B)\\) for the ideal generated by all \\(j\times j\\) minors of \\(B\\).

The exact rank-\\(\rho\\) stratum is

\\[ \boxed{ \mathcal S\_\rho = V(I\_{\rho+1}(B))\setminus V(I\_\rho(B)). } \tag{2} \\]

Here:

- \\(I\_{\rho+1}(B)=0\\) imposes rank at most \\(\rho\\);
- \\(I\_\rho(B)\ne0\\) means that at least one \\(\rho\\)-minor is nonzero, imposing rank at least \\(\rho\\).

The second condition is an open condition. It is a union of basic open sets, one for each possible nonzero \\(\rho\\)-minor. Your current implementation treats those possibilities separately, which explains the large number of witness-minor computations.

There is a standard way to avoid that enumeration when the question is emptiness.

Suppose \\(J\subset R\\) is the ideal containing the polynomial equations defining all the closed conditions we want to impose on the stratum. Then the relevant constructible set is

\\[ V(J)\setminus V(I\_\rho(B)). \\]

Its emptiness is equivalent to

\\[ \boxed{ J:I\_\rho(B)^\infty=R. } \tag{3} \\]

Here

\\[ J:I^\infty = \bigcup\_{k\ge0}(J:I^k) \\]

is the saturation of \\(J\\) by \\(I\\).

The reason is that saturation removes the irreducible components of \\(V(J)\\) that are entirely contained in \\(V(I)\\). The remaining variety is the Zariski closure of the part of \\(V(J)\\) lying outside \\(V(I)\\). Thus it is empty exactly when the constructible part is empty.

This replaces the disjunction over individual nonzero minors by one saturation with respect to the ideal generated by all \\(\rho\\)-minors.

For your problem, if the \\((5a)\\)-conditions have been expressed by equations in \\(J_a\\), the rank-\\(\rho\\) feasibility question becomes

\\[ \boxed{ J_a+I\_{\rho+1}(B) : I\_\rho(B)^\infty = R? } \tag{4} \\]

A unit ideal certifies that no algebraically admissible point exists on this rank stratum.

Important limitation: saturation is an algebraic emptiness tool. It gives the closure of the constructible set, not a complete description of its points. If it is nonempty, one still needs to retain the nonvanishing condition and produce a point satisfying it. For a rational witness, the point must also be rational; algebraic nonemptiness alone does not guarantee that.

### 2.2 The same idea can handle both rank strata

Your second stratification fixes

\\[ r(\theta)=\operatorname{rank}[\mathcal Z^\perp\mid W], \\]

in order to impose \\((5a)\\).

If you insist on expressing \\((5a)\\) through rank equality, then define

\\[ B_a(\theta)=[X(\theta)\mid W], \qquad T_a(\theta)=[X(\theta)A^{\mathsf T}\mid B_a(\theta)], \\]

where the columns of \\(X(\theta)\\) span the row space \\(\mathcal Z^\perp\\) in column coordinates.

The condition \\((5a)\\) is

\\[ \operatorname{rank}T_a(\theta) = \operatorname{rank}B_a(\theta). \\]

On a fixed rank-\\(r\\) stratum, this is expressed by

\\[ I\_{r+1}(B_a)=0,\qquad I\_{r+1}(T_a)=0, \\]

together with \\(I_r(B_a)\ne0\\).

The simultaneous \\(\rho,r\\) stratum can therefore be treated by

\\[ J\_{\rho,r} = I\_{\rho+1}(B)+I\_{r+1}(B_a)+I\_{r+1}(T_a), \\]

and one saturation:

\\[ \boxed{ J\_{\rho,r}: \big(I\_\rho(B)I_r(B_a)\big)^\infty. } \tag{5} \\]

Here the product denotes the product of ideals. Its nonvanishing locus is the intersection of the two open rank conditions.

This is a more economical algebraic formulation than separately adjoining a Rabinowitsch variable for every candidate witness minor. Whether it is computationally cheaper in SymPy is another question: ideal saturation by a large determinantal ideal can itself be expensive. But it is the right formulation to test before abandoning the approach.

### 2.3 A potentially better improvement: encode \\((5a)\\) by an inclusion, not by a rank stratum

I would actually prioritize this alternative.

Recall that

\\[ X=\mathcal Z^\perp =\operatorname{row}[C;\mathcal L], \qquad W=\operatorname{row}[C;CA]. \\]

Under the full-row-rank assumptions used in the project, condition \\((5a)\\) is equivalent to

\\[ \boxed{ XA\subseteq X+W. } \tag{6} \\]

In column coordinates, if \\(X_c\\) and \\(W_c\\) are basis matrices for the corresponding column spaces, this becomes

\\[ \operatorname{col}(A^{\mathsf T}X_c) \subseteq \operatorname{col}[X_c\mid W_c]. \\]

An inclusion of column spaces can be expressed by the existence of a coefficient matrix \\(T\\):

\\[ \boxed{ A^{\mathsf T}X_c = [X_c\mid W_c]T. } \tag{7} \\]

Once the chart basis matrices are substituted, (7) is a system of polynomial equations in the chart parameters and the auxiliary entries of \\(T\\), provided the chart bases have been chosen polynomially (or after clearing chart denominators while retaining their nonvanishing conditions).

This is important because it does not require fixing

\\[ r=\operatorname{rank}[X_c\mid W_c]. \\]

The existence of \\(T\\) expresses the inclusion directly.

Thus I would test the following formulation:

- stratify only by \\(\rho=\operatorname{rank}[V\mid Z]\\);
- encode \\((5a)\\) through the auxiliary matrix equation (7);
- encode the spectral condition using \\(M\_\rho(\theta)\\);
- impose the rank-\\(\rho\\) stratum by a single ideal saturation.

That removes the second rank stratification at the level of the mathematical formulation. It does not guarantee that the resulting Gröbner computation will be cheap, but it avoids an unnecessary source of combinatorial branching.

### 2.4 Is option (b), restricting to \\(h=n\\), legitimate?

It is legitimate as a sufficient subcase, but not as a completeness argument.

If

\\[ h=\operatorname{rank}[X;W]=n, \\]

then

\\[ X+W=\mathbb C^n. \\]

Consequently,

\\[ XA\subseteq X+W \\]

holds automatically, so \\((5a)\\) is automatic.

But this does not show that every admissible augmentation can be represented with \\(h=n\\). In fact, your previously established \\(n=7,q=3\\) witness has

\\[ h=5\<n=7 \\]

and satisfies both conditions. Restricting to \\(h=n\\) would discard that valid solution.

Therefore:

- using \\(h=n\\) to find a witness is legitimate;
- using \\(h=n\\) to prove infeasibility of the original problem is not legitimate unless you separately prove that every feasible augmentation can be replaced by one with \\(h=n\\).

I do not know such a replacement theorem in your setting, and the existing \\(h\<n\\) witness shows that \\(h=n\\) is not a necessary condition.

Recommendation for Q1

Use the \\((5a)\\) inclusion equation (7), and replace individual witness-minor enumeration by saturation with the determinantal ideal \\(I\_\rho(B)\\). Keep \\(h=n\\) as an optional fast sufficient case, never as the complete search space.

## 3. Q2 — Which compact representation should you investigate first?

My recommendation is unambiguous:

\\[ \boxed{\text{First investigate the implicit polynomial-matrix formulation.}} \\]

I would not start with explicit generators for the maximal-minor ideal, and I would not begin with an abstract framework. The implicit formulation preserves the special structure of your problem and avoids expanding the exponentially many minors.

### 3.1 Why the explicit maximal-minor representation should be abandoned as the main algorithm

Your measurements are convincing. At \\(n=28\\), the number of maximal minors is already approximately \\(1.7\times10^9\\), and the number of Grassmann charts is approximately \\(10^7\\). 【turn7file0】

This rules out a practical algorithm that explicitly enumerates every chart and every maximal minor at these dimensions.

It also means that the earlier Bézout coefficient-matrix formulation, although mathematically valid on a fixed rank stratum, is not a polynomial-size representation in the general input dimensions.

There is, however, an important distinction:

> The exponential size of this particular formulation does not prove that the decision problem is outside \\(\exists\mathbb R\\).

It proves only that this explicit maximal-minor/Bézout encoding does not establish membership in \\(\exists\mathbb R\\). A different polynomial-size encoding might still exist.

Similarly, the number of charts being exponential proves that chart enumeration is not a polynomial-time strategy. It does not prove that the underlying decision problem has no polynomial-time algorithm.

### 3.2 Keep the pencil implicit

For a fixed augmentation, the relevant polynomial matrix is

\\[ P_Z(\lambda) = [(\lambda I-A)V\mid Z]. \\]

Rather than constructing all of its maximal minors, work directly with this polynomial matrix.

For a fixed numerical parameter \\(\theta\\), the following are polynomial-matrix computations:

- compute its normal rank over \\(\mathbb Q(\lambda)\\);
- compute its determinantal divisors or Smith form;
- determine whether it has a finite rank drop;
- equivalently, determine whether its rank remains equal to the required rank for all finite \\(\lambda\\).

The key point is that polynomial-matrix algorithms operate on the matrix entries, not on an explicitly listed family of all maximal minors. They can therefore avoid the combinatorial expansion that has made the current approach infeasible.

For a fixed augmentation, I would use the following hierarchy:

1. Check the normal rank of \\(P_Z(\lambda)\\).
2. Verify that it equals the required rank \\(\rho\\).
3. Compute the relevant polynomial-matrix invariant factors or determinantal divisor.
4. Check whether the finite elementary-divisor contribution is absent.

This is consistent with your existing use of the maximal-minor gcd, but it does not require explicitly storing every maximal minor.

### 3.3 The difficult part is parameter dependence

The situation changes when \\(\theta\\) is variable.

For a fixed \\(\theta\\), polynomial-matrix algorithms can decide the spectral condition. But the per-order decision problem asks whether there exists a parameter \\(\theta\\) satisfying both the algebraic and spectral conditions.

Thus the real problem is not simply to test the rank of a polynomial matrix. It is to characterize the parameter values for which its rank profile has the required property.

I would investigate this in two stages.

Stage A: fixed-parameter structural algorithm.

Replace the current maximal-minor implementation by a polynomial-matrix algorithm for each fixed candidate augmentation. Validate it against:

- the Toeplitz test;
- the existing maximal-minor gcd;
- the direct nullspace-pencil test;
- the original pencil \\(Q\_{\mathcal L}(\lambda)\\).

This should make witness verification more efficient and reduce dependence on a large explicit list of minors.

Stage B: parametric structural formulation.

Study the matrix \\(P\_{Z(\theta)}(\lambda)\\) as a polynomial matrix over the rational-function field

\\[ \mathbb Q(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k)[\lambda]. \\]

The generic Smith form, determinantal divisors, and rank conditions describe the generic parameter point. But, as you have already discovered, they do not decide all exceptional specializations.

The next question is whether the exceptional specialization locus can be described through a polynomial-size representation of the relevant polynomial-matrix rank conditions, without explicitly expanding every maximal minor.

This is where I would look for a new theorem or algorithm.

### 3.4 How I rank the four possible approaches

| Approach                                       | My assessment                                                                              | Recommendation           |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------ |
| Explicit generators of the maximal-minor ideal | Mathematically direct, but risks reproducing the exponential expansion                     | Do not prioritize        |
| Generic linear combinations and resultants     | Useful for theoretical formulations, but parameter-dependent degree drops remain difficult | Keep as a secondary tool |
| Implicit polynomial-matrix formulation         | Preserves structure and avoids explicit enumeration                                        | First priority           |
| Abstract algebraic framework                   | Potentially valuable after the structural formulation is understood                        | Defer                    |

The resultant approach is not wrong. Its weakness is that it does not automatically eliminate the combinatorial number of minors. A generic linear combination of all maximal minors still has to be represented, and the parameter-dependent specialization problem remains.

The implicit pencil is the more promising starting point because it retains the structure that produced the pencil in the first place.

## 4. Q3 — Is the generic-gcd-degree dichotomy a known invariant of \\((V,W)\\)?

Not of the pair \\((V,W)\\) alone. There is a meaningful algebraic invariant here, but it belongs to the polynomial-matrix family over a specified parameter space, not merely to the two subspaces \\(V\\) and \\(W\\).

### 4.1 What the generic gcd degree actually measures

Let \\(X\\) be an irreducible algebraic parameter space for the admissible nullspaces on a chart, with function field

\\[ \mathbb K(X). \\]

Over this field, consider the polynomial matrix

\\[ P_Z(\lambda) = [(\lambda I-A)V\mid Z]. \\]

On a rank-\\(\rho\\) stratum, take the gcd of its \\(\rho\\)-minors:

\\[ \Delta\_\rho(\lambda) = \gcd\\{\text{all }\rho\text{-minors of }P_Z(\lambda)\\} \in \mathbb K(X)[\lambda], \\]

defined up to multiplication by a nonzero element of \\(\mathbb K(X)\\).

Then

\\[ \deg\_\lambda\Delta\_\rho \\]

is a well-defined invariant of this polynomial-matrix family over \\(\mathbb K(X)\\).

In the language of polynomial matrices, it is the degree of the corresponding determinantal divisor. Equivalently, it records the total finite elementary-divisor contribution at the generic parameter point.

So there is a standard algebraic object behind the observation. But the precise object is:

\\[ \boxed{ \text{the generic determinantal divisor of }P_Z(\lambda) \text{ over the function field of the chosen family.} } \\]

It is not, in general, an invariant of \\((V,W)\\) alone.

### 4.2 Why \\((V,W)\\) alone is insufficient

The pencil contains

\\[ (\lambda I-A)V. \\]

Consequently, it depends on the action of \\(A\\) on \\(V\\), not just on the subspaces \\(V\\) and \\(W\\).

The parameter family also depends on the allowed nullspaces

\\[ \mathcal Z\subseteq K, \\]

where \\(K=\ker[C;L_0]\\), and on the algebraically admissible subset selected by \\((5a)\\).

Thus the relevant data include at least:

- the matrix \\(A\\), especially its action on \\(V\\);
- the subspace \\(V\\);
- the allowed ambient subspace \\(K\\);
- the subspace \\(W\\);
- the algebraically admissible parameter family;
- the irreducible component under consideration.

A change in \\(A|\_V\\) can change the polynomial pencil while leaving \\(V\\) and \\(W\\) unchanged. Hence no invariant depending only on \\((V,W)\\) can capture the entire generic spectral behaviour.

### 4.3 The more precise geometric picture

I would define the algebraically admissible family on a chart by

\\[ X\_{\mathrm{alg}} = \\{\theta:\ (5a)\text{ holds and all chart/rank conditions hold}\\}. \\]

It may be reducible. Write its irreducible decomposition as

\\[ X\_{\mathrm{alg}} = X_1\cup\cdots\cup X_s. \\]

On each component \\(X_j\\), compute the generic determinantal divisor

\\[ \Delta\_{\rho,j}(\lambda) \in\mathbb K(X_j)[\lambda]. \\]

Then there are two generic cases on each component:

- If \\(\deg\Delta\_{\rho,j}=0\\), the spectral condition holds at the generic point of that component.
- If \\(\deg\Delta\_{\rho,j}\ge1\\), the generic point is spectrally infeasible.

This is the mathematically clean version of your observed dichotomy.

But there is an additional subtlety: the generic degree is component-dependent. A single chart can contain multiple irreducible components with different generic gcd degrees. Reporting one generic degree for the whole chart can therefore obscure the actual structure.

### 4.4 Does generic degree zero imply \\(X\_{\rm sp}=X\\)?

Not automatically.

If the generic gcd degree is zero, then, on an irreducible component, the spectral condition holds generically. This implies that there is a nonempty Zariski-open subset of that component on which the relevant rank condition holds.

It does not imply that every point of the component is feasible.

For example, a polynomial matrix can have full required rank at generic parameter values but lose rank at a special parameter value. The generic rank calculation cannot rule out such a special rank drop.

Therefore distinguish:

1. Generic feasibility: the spectral condition holds on a nonempty Zariski-open subset.
2. Universal feasibility: the spectral condition holds at every point.
3. Exceptional infeasibility: the spectral condition fails on a proper subset.

Your two reported examples may indeed satisfy universal feasibility on every chart. But that is stronger than generic gcd degree zero. To justify the stronger claim, verify the relevant Bézout coefficient matrix has full row rank at every admissible point, not merely at a generic point.

### 4.5 Is the feasible locus open or closed?

It is generally safer to call it constructible.

The finite-rank condition can be expressed using polynomial equalities and inequalities, so the spectral-feasible set is constructible. It need not be open or closed in the ambient parameter space.

The simple family

\\[ f(\theta,\lambda)=\theta\lambda+1,\qquad g(\theta,\lambda)=2\theta\lambda+2 \\]

illustrates the point. For \\(\theta\ne0\\), the two polynomials have a common root. At \\(\theta=0\\), they become \\(1\\) and \\(2\\), so their gcd becomes \\(1\\). The good locus is the closed set \\(\\{\theta=0\\}\\), not an open set.

This is exactly the kind of degree drop that your report describes.

Thus I would replace the informal two-class description by:

> On each irreducible component of the algebraically admissible parameter family, the generic determinantal-divisor degree determines generic spectral feasibility. Exceptional feasible points in a generically infeasible component form a constructible subset that is not dense in that component.

That is a precise statement, provided the spectral condition has been expressed correctly on the fixed rank stratum.

### 4.6 Does this explain the thinness at \\(q_0\\)?

It gives a framework for explaining it, but does not yet prove a general minimum-order theorem.

At \\(q_0\\), the algebraic admissibility locus may be geometrically constrained. It is possible that the generic pencil on a component has a positive-degree determinantal divisor, while exceptional specializations remove it.

However, there is no general reason, from the definition of \\(q_0\\) alone, that every component must have positive generic gcd degree. Your report's own examples distinguish generic degree zero from generic degree positive.

My recommendation is to study the generic determinantal-divisor degree componentwise, rather than to search for a universal theorem that the feasible set at \\(q_0\\) is always thin.

## 5. Q4 — Are the \\((5a)\\) and spectral conditions complementary?

No general complementarity should be expected. They are distinct conditions, and the point in II.4 is a natural illustration of their independence.

Recall the geometric form of the algebraic condition:

\\[ (5a) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad XA\subseteq X+W, \qquad X=\mathcal Z^\perp. \\]

The spectral condition is expressed through

\\[ P_Z(\lambda)=[(\lambda I-A)V\mid Z]. \\]

These conditions use related data, but they test different things.

- \\((5a)\\) asks whether the action of \\(A\\) on the row-space augmentation \\(X\\) stays inside \\(X+W\\).
- The nullspace-pencil condition asks whether the pencil formed from \\((\lambda I-A)V\\) and \\(Z\\) maintains the required rank at every finite \\(\lambda\\).

There is no immediate implication in either direction from these definitions alone.

### 5.1 What the \\(n=6\\) degenerate point demonstrates

Your report says that at \\(\theta=5/4\\):

- \\(\operatorname{rank}[V\mid Z]=2\\);
- the corresponding spectral coefficient matrix has full row rank;
- the spectral rank test is satisfied;
- \\((5a)\\) fails.

This is an important observation. It shows that the spectral rank condition can hold at a point that is not algebraically admissible. 【turn7file0】

But the correct conclusion needs a little care.

The nullspace-pencil criterion was derived for the jointly admissible setting, where \\((5a)\\) is assumed. Therefore, if you use that criterion at a point where \\((5a)\\) fails, you should call it the nullspace-pencil rank condition, not automatically claim that the original \\((5b)\\) has been proved.

To make the report completely rigorous, check the original pencil

\\[ Q\_{\mathcal L}(\lambda) = [\lambda\mathcal L-\mathcal LA;CA;C] \\]

directly at \\(\theta=5/4\\), or state explicitly that the observation concerns the auxiliary nullspace-pencil condition before imposing \\((5a)\\).

This distinction does not weaken the main conclusion. It makes it more precise.

### 5.2 Why the two conditions do not reduce to one rank condition

A tempting strategy would be to find a rank formulation in which one condition automatically implies the other. The example suggests that this is not generally possible.

The inclusion

\\[ XA\subseteq X+W \\]

is a linear-algebraic incidence condition on the row-space augmentation.

The spectral condition is a polynomial-pencil condition. It involves all \\(\lambda\\), and it detects finite elementary divisors or rank drops.

Even if both are expressible through determinantal equations, they are not the same determinantal condition. The fact that two conditions can each be expressed by minors does not make one a consequence of the other.

I would therefore keep the two layers separate in the algorithm:

1. impose \\((5a)\\) as a column-space inclusion or rank condition;
2. impose the spectral condition on the algebraically admissible subset.

The inclusion formulation (7) from Q1 is especially useful here because it avoids the second rank stratification without pretending that the spectral condition implies algebraic admissibility.

## 6. Q5 — Should per-order decision remain the top priority?

My recommendation is to prioritize writing up the two established mathematical results, while continuing the per-order decision problem as a narrower technical project.

I would not abandon the decision problem. But the report has reached a natural point at which further computational work risks consuming substantial time without producing a theorem of comparable strength.

### 6.1 Why the two results should now be written up

The report identifies two results with a clear mathematical message:

1. the dimension obstruction and its uniformity under the stated hypothesis;
2. the \\(n=6\\) counterexample showing whole-family spectral infeasibility beyond what the dimension obstruction detects.

The counterexample is especially valuable because it demonstrates that the dimension obstruction is not a complete characterization. The two independent proofs strengthen it.

These results do not depend on completing the general per-order decision algorithm.

I would prepare a self-contained draft with:

- exact definitions and hypotheses;
- full rank and nullspace calculations;
- the dimension obstruction theorem;
- the uniformity result;
- the exact \\(n=6\\) data;
- both proofs of the counterexample;
- a short computational reproducibility appendix.

The short note should not claim a general algorithm for \\(q\_{\min}\\), nor should it present the six-instance generic-gcd observation as a theorem.

### 6.2 What should continue in the per-order programme?

I would retain two focused tasks.

Task A: improve the algebraic formulation.

Try the combined approach:

\\[ \text{\\((5a)\\) as a column-space inclusion} \quad+\quad \text{one rank-\\(\rho\\) stratum} \quad+\quad \text{ideal saturation}. \\]

This is the most direct way to test whether the double-stratification bottleneck can be reduced.

Task B: improve fixed-witness spectral verification.

Implement a polynomial-matrix algorithm for the fixed-augmentation spectral test. This should improve reliability and efficiency independently of the general existence problem.

I would not currently spend a large amount of time trying to force the full chart/minor enumeration to scale. The measurements already establish that this representation is unsuitable at the tested dimensions.

### 6.3 What I would postpone

I would postpone:

- a hardness reduction;
- a universal theorem about the dimension of the \\(q_0\\) spectral-feasible locus;
- a broad companion-form investigation.

These may become valuable later, but they are not prerequisites for writing up the results already established.
